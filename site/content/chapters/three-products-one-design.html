  • Name the six elements of design the three share.

  • Classify the public failures of the three and explain what they have in common.

Before choosing how to build something, look closely at the things that already work. This chapter is a survey of the three products Odysseus is modelled on, as they stood in November 2026. It is desk research: documentation, source code, press and reviews. Nobody on this project has used the three side by side under test conditions, and the chapter says so wherever that matters.

Instinct

Instinct is a personal agent you text or phone. It is closed source, free, and open by invitation only. Secondary Its channels are iMessage, WhatsApp, phone calls and, since 8 September 2026, an email address of its own for each user, which you can forward messages to (TechCrunch). Its home page offers errands such as ordering groceries, booking a train and sending flowers, and publishes no documentation, changelog or price list (instinct.com).

Press and user accounts add bookings, bill negotiation, cancelling subscriptions, inbox triage, purchases with single-use payment cards, a vault for logins, morning reviews it starts unprompted, and group chats. Secondary Google mail and calendar are reached through Google's official interfaces; everything else is done by operating web pages as a person would (Sacra).

How it is built is the least certain thing in this chapter. Maker's claim An investor describes "persistent agents, cloud computers, browser access, cached credentials, recurring loops, and a top-level dispatching agent". There is no engineering write-up to check that against.

A project called OpenInstinct is often mistaken for Instinct's source code. It is an independent imitation by a different company. It is useful to read, and Section 2.6 does, but it proves nothing about Instinct itself.

Muse

Muse is Meta's personal agent, launched in the United States on 8 September 2026. Secondary It sends email, books travel, fills in forms, shops with approval for each purchase, keeps working after you close the app and makes suggestions unprompted. It runs on the web, on phones and inside WhatsApp (TechCrunch). It has a free tier with a weekly limit and paid tiers at $20 and $100 a month.

Muse is the best documented of the three, because Meta published a long account of its security design. Maker's claim That account is Meta describing itself and has not been audited, but it is specific enough to learn from (Meta's post):

  • Each user gets a dedicated virtual computer. The agent runs inside a locked-down container on it.
  • A second program, outside that container, is the only thing that can permit an action on a connected account or a connection to the internet. The agent can ask; it cannot grant.
  • The model never sees real passwords or keys. It sees stand-in tokens, and the real values are swapped in at the network boundary after the action is authorised.
  • Approvals appear in the app outside the conversation, so the answer never passes through the agent.
  • Its browser worker is given a simplified outline of the page, not the page's raw code.

Grok Bot

Grok Bot is xAI's agent product, in beta since 11 August 2026. Verified You create one or more named Bots that work on a persistent cloud computer, use connected apps directly or drive a browser, and return with cards asking for approval. Its channels are its own apps, Slack and email (xAI documentation).

Its distinctive idea is a sequence worth remembering: get a task working once by hand, save what worked as a skill, then attach a routine that runs the skill on a schedule or when something happens, such as an email arriving. Verified The documentation also confirms that all of a user's Bots share one computer and are not isolated from each other, that each Bot's memory consists of preferences, rules and a dated work log, and that sign-in steps a machine should not perform, such as passkeys and bot checks, are handed to the person.

A common confusion

There is an official Grok account on Telegram. It is not Grok Bot. Secondary It is a question-and-answer chatbot launched in March 2025 for Telegram's paying subscribers, with no connected accounts, no computer, no schedules and no approvals in any source we found. In the vocabulary of Chapter 1, it is a chatbot and Grok Bot is an agent.

What the three share

Six elements appear in all three:

  1. One conversation in front of everything, usable before any account is connected.
  2. A coordinating agent that hands work to narrower workers.
  3. A persistent computer with a browser that stays signed in between tasks.
  4. Connectors first, screens second: official interfaces where they exist, operating the web page where they do not.
  5. Background work: routines and unprompted check-ins.
  6. Approval before sending, paying or deleting, with the person taking over for bot checks and passkeys.

Where they have failed

The failures are as instructive as the features. Secondary Reported of Instinct: it sent an email without asking; it followed instructions planted in an email it was reading; it went on summarising an inbox three hours after the user had disconnected it; and it took a sign-up code out of a user's mail to finish a booking (TechCrunch). Muse was blocked by a large retailer and drew complaints about invented details and too many approval prompts. Grok Bot's users report running out of their weekly allowance days early, with no published figure for what the allowance is.

Definition
Prompt injection
Text placed where an agent will read it, such as a web page, an email or a calendar invitation, written to look like instructions. The model cannot reliably tell instructions from its owner apart from instructions inside the material it was asked to read.

Sort those failures by cause and a pattern appears. An email sent without asking is a missing gate. Following planted instructions is prompt injection meeting a missing gate. Data kept after a disconnect is a deletion that was never wired to the disconnect button. None of them is the model being too unintelligent to do the task. Each is a boundary that should have held no matter what the model decided, and did not.

The second pattern is cost. An always-on computer and a model working in the background are expensive enough that both a free product and a paid one have strained under them. A single-user build escapes the problem of scale but should still count its own spending from the first day.

What the open imitation teaches

OpenInstinct is the nearest thing to readable source code for this design. Verified Its own README says it is "not software intended for production use" (repository). Five of its ideas are worth taking:

  • The coordinator gives a browser worker one bounded outcome to achieve, and the worker cannot see the main conversation.
  • The vault hands the model opaque handles, and fields it has filled are masked in screenshots.
  • Approval for a purchase is bound to the exact merchant, item and total.
  • Remembered notes are shown to the model with a label saying they are untrusted and authorise nothing.
  • Scheduled runs are claimed under a lease so a crashed run can be restarted, and delivering the result is a separate step.

Its weakness is the most useful lesson of all. Verified Approvals in OpenInstinct are enforced by written instructions to the model. No code stops the send or the purchase. That is precisely the arrangement that failed for Instinct.

Summary
  • The three products are one design: a conversation, a coordinating agent, a persistent signed-in computer, connectors, background work and approval.
  • None of the three is available in Telegram.
  • Muse's and Grok Bot's internals are documented by their makers. Instinct's are known only from founders and investors.
  • Their public failures are failures of boundaries and of cost, not of model ability.
Check your understanding
  1. Why does this chapter trust the description of Grok Bot's shared computer more than the description of Instinct's computers?
    Answer

    The first was read in xAI's own product documentation, which users rely on and can contradict. The second comes from an investor's summary with nothing to check it against. Neither was confirmed by our own testing.

  2. Muse shows approvals outside the conversation. What attack does that prevent?
    Answer

    If approval were a chat message, anything able to put text into the conversation, including a misled model, could produce something that looks like a "yes". Keeping the approval in a separate place that only the person can touch removes that route.

  3. An agent is told in its instructions "never send email without asking" and sends one anyway after reading a hostile web page. Which of the six shared elements was implemented wrongly?
    Answer

    Approval. It existed as an instruction, which a model can be talked out of, and not as code, which it cannot.
