Definition
Stack
The full set of parts a system is assembled from: which model, which hosting, which libraries, which outside services.

Chapter 3 named the parts without naming suppliers. This chapter names them. It also tells the story of how the choice was made, including two proposals that were replaced within hours, because the way a decision changed teaches more than the final table does.

Three rounds in one day

Round one: cheapest that works. The first proposal ran everything on one small rented server: the bot, the agent, our own copy of Chrome, memory in plain files, and a mid-priced model. Its reasoning was that the survey in Chapter 2 found the failures of the reference products in the code around the model, so money spent on a cleverer model or a hosted browser would be spent in the wrong place.

Round two: best performing. The owner rejected that proposal with an instruction to choose the most performant vendors for the goal. The parts were chosen again with one question for each: what is the best available evidence of who performs best? This moved three choices: the top-priced model in place of the mid-priced one, a hosted browser in place of our own, and one connector service for every app.

Round three: one reversal. The owner accepted round two except for the model, where the mid-priced choice from round one was restored. The stated reason was cost: the top model's list price is four times higher, and nobody has published what a month of running an assistant like this costs.

The full sequence is in the build log, entries 003 and 004, in the order it happened.

Round three is defensible on the evidence, not only on price. The model maker's own guidance is to use the cheapest model that passes your tests and to adjust how hard it thinks before switching to a dearer one. Odysseus has no tests yet. Starting with the dearest model would be paying to solve a problem that has not been observed.

How to read a benchmark

Round two depended on benchmarks, and most of the benchmarks available turned out to be run by the companies they rank. That does not make them useless. It means they must be read with four questions in mind.

  1. Who ran it? A company testing itself chooses the conditions.
  2. How were the test cases chosen? A sample picked where one product is known to do well measures the picking.
  3. Who judged the answers? When a model marks the work, its mistakes become part of the score.
  4. Has anyone else reproduced it? A number that only its owner can obtain is a claim.
Worked example: the four questions applied

A memory product. Maker's claim One vendor of memory for agents reports scores above 92% on two standard tests (its report). Secondary An independent test of the same vendor's open-source edition reported 32.4% on one of them (write-up). The two tested different editions with different settings, so neither figure settles the matter, but question 4 has an answer: the headline number was not reproduced. A separate audit reportedly found that the automated judge on a widely used memory test accepts a large share of deliberately wrong answers, which is question 3.

A browser service. Maker's claim One hosted-browser company publishes a test of how often each vendor's browser gets past bot defences, and ranks itself first at 90.5% (its comparison). Its own write-up says the sites were chosen where its product had historically succeeded and ordinary Chrome had not. That is question 2, answered candidly by the company itself. The ranking of the other vendors in that test is more believable than the top line, because the company had no reason to favour one rival over another.

Two choices where no vendor won

The instruction was to choose the most performant vendors. For memory and for scheduling the honest finding was that no vendor performs better than a simple thing we can build. Three independent systems studied in the research have arrived at the same shape for memory: a short file that is always loaded, dated notes that are searched when needed, a save just before the conversation is condensed, and a tidy-up that runs later. That agreement is better evidence than any single score. Choosing a vendor here to satisfy the wording of the instruction would have defeated its purpose.

Why not simply run a ready-made agent

Open-source personal agents exist that already provide a Telegram conversation, file memory and a scheduler. Running one would produce something usable in an afternoon. The reason for building is the argument of Chapter 3: the gate, the vault and the delivery of results are the parts that decide whether the system can be trusted, and those are the parts we need to own and understand. A ready-made agent remains a sensible baseline to measure against in Chapter 14.

What we are least sure of

  1. The browser vendor. This is the weakest choice in the table. One rival is the only vendor that scores well on a test it did not run. The choice will be settled by running both on the sites the owner actually uses.
  2. Bot checks. Hosted browsers offer to solve bot checks automatically. The plan is to leave that off and pass each check to the owner through a live view of the browser. This will cost some tasks that could otherwise finish unattended, and it is a decision about conduct, not performance.
  3. The model maker. The comparison leans towards one maker. Part of the reason is that its documentation was the easiest to read at the source, which is a fact about our research and not about its models.
  4. The monthly bill. No builder has published the running cost of a single-user assistant. Ours will be known after the first slice has run for a week.
A declared conflict

The AI coding agent that helps build Odysseus and drafts these pages runs on a model from the same maker chosen in the table above. It recommended that maker. Readers should weigh the recommendation by the evidence cited, and the comparison against another maker's models is on the list for Chapter 14.

Summary
  • The stack was chosen three times in a day: for cost, then for performance, then with the model returned to the mid-priced choice.
  • Most available benchmarks were run by the companies they rank. Ask who ran it, how cases were chosen, who judged, and whether it was reproduced.
  • Vendors were chosen for the browser, connectors, search and hosting. For memory and scheduling no vendor outperformed a simple build.
  • The browser choice is the least certain and will be decided by our own test.
Check your understanding
  1. A vendor's benchmark ranks itself first and three rivals second to fourth. Which part of the ranking deserves more trust, and why?
    Answer

    The order of the rivals. The vendor has an interest in its own position but usually none in which rival comes second, so that part is less likely to be shaped by the choice of test cases.

  2. Why was the mid-priced model restored even though a dearer one tops a public leaderboard?
    Answer

    The leaderboard measures operating a computer in general, not this owner's errands. Odysseus has no tests of its own yet, the price difference is fourfold, and the monthly cost is unknown. The dearer model can be adopted when a measured gap justifies it.

  3. The instruction was "choose the most performant vendors", yet memory uses no vendor. Is that a failure to follow the instruction?
    Answer

    No. The purpose of the instruction is performance. Where the evidence shows a simple build performing as well as or better than the products on sale, the simple build is the most performant choice.
